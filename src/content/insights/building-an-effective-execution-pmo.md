Every private equity investment starts with a thesis about why the business will be worth more at exit than at entry. That thesis usually becomes a value creation plan, a set of initiatives meant to close the gap between the entry and exit numbers.

Writing the plan tends to go fairly smoothly. Delivering it is harder. Management teams still have a business to run, initiatives compete for the same people, progress gets reported in different ways, and the board ends up seeing a lot of activity without a clear sense of results. Six months in, it can be hard to tell which parts of the plan are working.

An execution program management office, or PMO, is meant to fix that. Set up well, it gives the plan structure, a regular rhythm and clear accountability. Set up poorly, it turns into a reporting exercise that management puts up with and the board stops reading.

## What the PMO should and shouldn't do

An execution PMO is a small team that makes sure the value creation plan gets delivered. It runs the cadence, keeps the data and manages escalations. The initiatives themselves should stay with the management team.

I think this distinction matters a lot. If the PMO takes over initiatives, it becomes a second management team and the company never builds the ability to deliver on its own. If it only collects status updates, it adds overhead without changing much. The model that works best is in between, with management owning delivery and the PMO helping it stay visible, coordinated and on schedule.

## Start with the investment thesis

One of the PMO's first jobs is to check that the plan actually reflects the thesis. Plans written under deal pressure sometimes include initiatives that are easy to describe but don't move the numbers much.

A useful exercise is to connect each initiative back to the entry model. Which line of the EBITDA bridge does it support? Is it about cost, margin, growth or building capability? What needs to be true for it to work, and how confident is the team that it is?

If an initiative can't be tied back to the thesis, it's worth questioning. And if there's a value lever in the thesis with no initiative behind it, that's a gap someone needs to own.

## Give every initiative a short charter

Each initiative should have a one- or two-page charter covering the same basics:

- What it will achieve, in specific terms
- Who owns it, by name
- The expected financial benefit, both run-rate and in-year, and when it should land
- The cost to achieve it, including people, technology and outside help
- Key milestones and dates
- Dependencies and risks
- The measures that will show whether it's working

Charters give everyone a common format. They also make it obvious which ideas aren't ready yet, because nobody can fill in the owner or the financial impact.

## Track initiatives by maturity

Some initiatives are well understood and ready to go, while others are still ideas that need testing. Reporting them as if they were equally certain gives the board a misleading picture.

A simple set of stages helps:

1. **Identified:** an opportunity with an estimated value
2. **Validated:** the value has been tested with data and confirmed or revised
3. **Approved:** a plan, owner and budget are in place
4. **In delivery:** work is underway against milestones
5. **Realized:** the benefit shows up in the financial results

Reporting value by stage shows how much of the plan is real, how much is in progress and how much still needs work.

## An example charter, from idea to realized value

To show how the charter and the stages fit together, here is a hypothetical initiative. The company, people and figures are invented for illustration and are not drawn from any client.

- **Initiative:** consolidate purchasing of indirect goods and services across three sites.
- **Owner:** Chief Operating Officer, with the procurement lead running day-to-day work.
- **Milestones:** new supplier agreements signed by month four; all three sites buying under them by month six.
- **Cost to achieve:** $150,000 for sourcing support and purchasing-system changes.
- **Target:** $600,000 in run-rate savings.

| Stage | What happens | How value is reported |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identified | Diligence notes that each site buys the same categories from different suppliers | Rough estimate of $500,000 to $800,000 a year |
| Validated | Finance pulls twelve months of spend by category and agrees the baseline with the COO | Target set at $600,000 run-rate |
| Approved | The charter is signed off with an owner, milestones and the $150,000 budget | Target, timing and cost to achieve locked in |
| In delivery | Agreements are signed for two of the three sites | $400,000 run-rate contracted; in-year savings tracked separately |
| Realized | Finance compares actual spend against the agreed baseline | $560,000 run-rate confirmed, slightly below target because volumes in one category fell |

Two details matter here. The savings were only counted as realized after finance checked them against the baseline agreed at the validation stage, and the shortfall against target was reported rather than smoothed over. Both make it easier for the board to trust the other numbers in the report.

## Set a meeting rhythm that leads to decisions

The cadence has a big effect on whether the PMO speeds the business up or slows it down. A typical setup is a weekly working session with initiative owners on progress and blockers, a monthly steering meeting with the CEO and leadership team for decisions and trade-offs, and quarterly board reporting against the plan.

A good check is whether each meeting ends with decisions made. If most of the time goes to status updates, those updates probably belong in a dashboard.

## Tie it back to the financials

The most common weakness I see in value creation reporting is a disconnect between initiative progress and the P&L. An initiative can be on schedule while the savings never appear, because the baseline moved, the cost shifted somewhere else or the same benefit was counted twice.

The PMO should work closely with finance to agree baselines up front, define how each benefit will be measured and regularly reconcile reported value with actual results. It's not the most exciting part of the work, but it's what gives the board confidence in the numbers.

## Staff it with experienced people

An effective PMO is usually small and senior. It needs someone with enough credibility to challenge executives, enough financial skill to test the numbers and enough operating experience to recognize what good execution looks like.

Many portfolio companies don't have that person in-house at the start of the hold period. Sponsors often bring in interim support to set up the PMO and get the rhythm going, with a plan to hand over to a permanent leader once it's running smoothly.

## Common pitfalls

A few patterns come up again and again: too many initiatives for the management team to handle, reporting that tracks activity instead of results, benefits counted before they're validated, a PMO that takes over delivery instead of supporting it, steering meetings spent on status updates, and no link between the PMO and the budgeting and forecasting process. Most of these can be avoided with a few deliberate choices at the start.

## A practical first 90 days

If you're setting up an execution PMO, a reasonable sequence looks like this:

1. **Weeks 1 to 3:** review the plan against the thesis, meet the initiative owners and agree on governance
2. **Weeks 4 to 6:** finish the charters, agree financial baselines and stages, and start the weekly cadence
3. **Weeks 7 to 12:** run the first steering meetings, deliver the first board report and adjust the portfolio based on what you've learned

By the end of that period, the company should have a plan people believe in, a routine that keeps it moving, and reporting the board trusts.
