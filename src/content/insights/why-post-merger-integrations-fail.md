Integrations don't usually fail all at once. More often they fall behind gradually. Decisions take longer than planned, a few synergy initiatives slip a quarter, some good people leave, and customers start to notice small changes in service. By the time the shortfall is visible in the numbers, the causes go back months.

When I look at integrations that struggled, many of those causes trace back to the first hundred days, and some to the weeks before close. That early period is when people in the combined organization figure out how decisions get made and whether the integration plan is something leadership really means. Below are the issues I see most often and what tends to help.

## Planning starts too late

Between signing and close, the deal team is usually focused on approvals, financing and closing documents. Integration planning often waits until the deal is certain, and the result is a Day 1 with a solid communications plan and not much else.

Where regulations allow it, the time between signing and close is valuable. It's a good window to agree on what the integration needs to achieve and how progress will be measured, sketch the target operating model even if the details come later, list the decisions that need to be made in the first month, prepare Day 1 readiness for people, systems, customers and suppliers, and name the integration leaders and free up their time.

Clean team arrangements can help when competitively sensitive information is involved. The goal is to reach close with a plan already in motion.

## Nobody is sure who decides

In a newly combined company, decision rights are unclear almost by default. Leaders from both sides have overlapping responsibilities, and the integration team often has accountability without much authority. Questions either escalate to the CEO or sponsor, or they sit.

It helps to spell out governance early: a steering committee that can make and enforce decisions, an integration management office to track decisions, risks and dependencies, workstream leads with clear scope, and a short list of decisions reserved for the executive team.

Rhythm matters as much as structure. Weekly working sessions and a regular steering meeting stop decisions from piling up.

## The synergy case never becomes a plan

During the deal, synergies are estimated at a level that works for valuation, such as a percentage of combined costs or a revenue uplift from cross-selling. That's a reasonable starting point for valuation, but it needs a lot more detail before anyone can deliver it.

Each synergy needs to turn into a specific initiative with an owner, a baseline, a timeline, a cost to achieve and a way to measure it. Without that step, synergies stay as a target everyone agrees with and no one is directly responsible for.

By the end of the first hundred days, you should have a validated synergy baseline and a list of initiatives with owners. Some estimates will go up and some will come down, and that's normal.

## Day 1 gets all the attention

A smooth Day 1 matters. Payroll has to run, customers need to be served, systems have to work, and employees need to know what's happening. But some teams put so much into Day 1 that there's very little planned for the weeks after.

A simple test is to ask the integration team what they'll be working on in week three, week six and week twelve. If the answers are vague, the program will probably lose pace once the business settles back into its routine. I've covered how to keep that cadence going in a separate piece on [building an execution PMO](/insights/building-an-effective-execution-pmo/).

## People are left guessing

Employees of an acquired company want to know whether their job is safe, who they'll report to and whether the things they liked about the company will survive. If they don't get answers, they'll fill in the gaps themselves, and the people with the most options usually move first.

A few things make a real difference: identifying critical people early and talking to them directly, being honest about what's been decided and what hasn't (and when it will be), making organizational decisions quickly instead of leaving them open for months, and paying attention to everyday details like system access and benefits.

In practice, culture is shaped by how all of these decisions are made and communicated, which makes it difficult to manage as a separate workstream.

## Customers feel the changes first

Customers don't care about the deal rationale. They care whether their account manager has changed, whether invoices are right and whether service is as good as before. Consolidating sales teams, migrating systems or changing pricing too quickly can create the kind of disruption competitors look for.

It's worth mapping key accounts early, being careful about any change customers will see, and watching early warning signs of churn rather than waiting for revenue to drop.

## The IT plan is too ambitious

Systems integration is often where synergy timelines slip. Moving everything onto one platform may be the right long-term goal, but it's expensive and slow, and not every integration needs it on the same timeline.

A practical approach separates what has to be connected on Day 1, what should be consolidated in the first year, and what can stay separate for now. The synergy plan and the operating model should drive that sequence. Issues found during [IT due diligence](/insights/it-due-diligence-red-flags/) are a good place to start.

## What good looks like at day 100

By the end of the first hundred days, a well-run integration should have:

- A governance model that's making decisions on a regular schedule
- A target operating model that's been communicated and is being put in place
- A synergy list with owners, baselines and tracked progress
- The critical people it set out to keep
- Stable customer metrics with no unexplained rise in churn
- A technology roadmap tied to the synergy and operating model plans
- A clear view of the remaining work and the resources to finish it

None of this guarantees success, but it puts the integration on much firmer ground.
