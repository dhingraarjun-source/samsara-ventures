IT diligence usually gets squeezed. It happens in the same few weeks as everything else, the data room is incomplete, and the people who really understand the systems are often not in the room. Under that kind of pressure, it is tempting to treat technology as a checklist item and move on.

In my experience, a lot of post-close surprises start there. Technology issues rarely stop a deal, but they often add cost and delay that nobody planned for. A core system that needs replacing, a business that depends on its parent's infrastructure, or a security problem that has to be fixed before anything else can move forward will all show up in the first year of ownership whether you priced them in or not.

You do not need to review every application to avoid this. What you need is a clear view of the handful of issues that could affect the investment case. These are the five I would look at first.

## 1. An aging or heavily customized core platform

Most companies depend on one or two systems for nearly everything, usually an ERP, an operational platform or a billing system. If those systems are old, out of vendor support or heavily customized, the business is carrying a cost that won't show up in the financials until someone tries to upgrade or replace them.

Things worth asking about:

- Which core systems are out of support today, or will be during the expected hold period?
- How much custom code has been built on top of packaged software?
- Are there multiple versions of the same system, often left over from past acquisitions?
- Have there been upgrade projects that were started and then put on hold?

What matters most is whether the platform can support the growth plan, and what it would cost if it can't. Replacing a core system usually takes years and carries real risk, so if the plan depends on acquisitions or fast growth, that cost belongs in the model.

## 2. Too much knowledge in too few heads

Many mid-market companies rely on a very small number of people who understand how the systems fit together. Sometimes it's a long-serving IT leader, and sometimes it's an outside contractor who built the original platform and still maintains it.

That's manageable under stable ownership. It becomes a risk when ownership changes and the business is expected to move faster. Key people may leave, or they may simply become a bottleneck.

Signs to look for include thin or outdated documentation, critical systems supported by one person or a small vendor without a proper contract, and an IT team that spends almost all of its time keeping things running. If management keeps deferring technical questions to the same person, take note.

Retention arrangements, documentation work and early hiring can all help, but they're much easier to plan before close than after.

## 3. Systems and contracts the business doesn't actually own

This comes up most often in carve-outs, though not only there. A division being sold may run on the parent's ERP, share its network and email, and use software licenses that can't be transferred to a new owner. In founder-led businesses, important tools are sometimes licensed under someone's personal account or bundled into an affiliate's contracts.

Every one of these needs to be replaced, transitioned or covered by a transition service agreement, and each has a cost and a timeline. It helps to build a simple list early in diligence:

- Which applications and infrastructure are shared with a parent or affiliate?
- Which software contracts have change-of-control or assignment restrictions?
- Where does the company's data sit, and who controls access to it?
- What will the seller provide under a TSA, for how long and at what cost?

Having this picture early gives the deal team a stronger position when negotiating standalone costs and TSA terms, and a realistic starting point for the separation plan. ## 4. Cybersecurity that hasn't been tested

Security has become a valuation issue. A breach during the hold period can bring regulatory problems, lost customers and expensive remediation, and the next buyer will run their own security review at exit.

Policies and questionnaires tell you what the company intends to do. It's more useful to ask for evidence of what actually happens. Is multi-factor authentication enforced for email, remote access and admin accounts? When were backups last restored as a test? Has there been a recent penetration test, and were the findings fixed? Have there been incidents, and how were they handled?

Gaps here don't always affect price, but they usually mean a set of actions that need a budget and an owner from day one.

## 5. IT spending that doesn't add up

Low IT spend can mean the business is efficient. It can also mean investment has been deferred, technology costs are sitting in other departments' budgets, or important work is being done informally by people whose main job is something else.

A few things to test: whether IT costs reconcile to the general ledger, how much technology spending happens outside central IT, how much labor and software development is being capitalized, how old the infrastructure is, and whether any vendor contracts auto-renew or reprice on a change of control.

What you're after is a realistic view of what it costs to run the technology this business needs, today and under the growth plan. That number is more useful in the model than the current budget line.

## A checklist for the data room

Here are the five red flags in one place, with the evidence worth asking for and the kind of decision each one can lead to. The right response always depends on the deal and the investment case, so treat the last column as a set of options rather than rules.

| Red flag | Evidence to request | Possible decision or action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aging or heavily customized core platform | System inventory with versions and vendor support dates, a list of major customizations, and the history of any paused upgrade projects | Include upgrade or replacement cost in the model, and adjust the timing of growth initiatives that depend on the platform |
| Knowledge held by a few people | Architecture and integration documentation, the IT organization chart, and contracts with key contractors | Retention or transition arrangements for key people, and documentation work early in the hold period |
| Systems and contracts the business doesn't own | A list of shared applications and infrastructure, software contracts with change-of-control or assignment terms, and the proposed TSA scope | Standalone cost estimate, TSA scope and duration agreed in the deal, and a separation plan |
| Untested cybersecurity | Evidence of multi-factor authentication, the last backup restore test, recent penetration test results and fixes, and incident history | First-100-day security actions with a budget and owner, and specific protections in the deal terms where needed |
| IT spending that doesn't add up | IT costs reconciled to the general ledger, technology spend outside central IT, capitalized labor and software development, and vendor renewal terms | A normalized run-rate IT cost in the model, plus an estimate of one-time investment |

## Turning findings into decisions

The most useful IT diligence report is short. It should tell the deal team whether there are any risks serious enough to affect the decision to proceed, what the business will need to invest in one-time and ongoing spend to support the plan, what has to happen in the first hundred days and who will own it, and whether anything should be reflected in the deal terms, such as price, indemnities, TSA terms or conditions to close.

Framed that way, the technology work feeds straight into the [value creation plan](/services/value-creation/) instead of sitting in a binder after signing.
